ALSEP: the station that kept listening

The problem

A crew could stay a few days. Scientists wanted to watch the Moon for weeks, even years. So the astronauts built a robot science station, switched it on, and left it running.

The machine

Instruments were arranged around a 25 kg central station that held the radio and data processors. The astronauts aimed its antenna at Earth, and engineers back home sent it commands. A plutonium-238 generator called SNAP-27 turned heat into about 70 watts, so the station never depended on sunlight.

What it heard

The seismometers recorded a huge number of moonquakes, some about 700 km down. Decades later, scientists re-read the old data and found the Moon seems to have a solid inner core inside a molten outer core, like Earth. When NASA crashed Apollo 12’s ascent stage into the Moon on purpose, the Moon rang like a struck bell for nearly an hour.

The goodbye

On 30 September 1977, NASA switched every station off. The goals had been met, and the extra data ranked below other needs for money. They were built to last about a year, yet all were still sending useful data five years after Apollo 12.

Still there

The equipment remains at five landing sites, and LRO has photographed the Apollo 12 station. Its legacy lives on in NASA’s new LEMS seismometer and the Farside Seismic Suite, planned to be up to 30 times more sensitive.

    The descent stage: a landing pad that became a launch pad

    Two halves

    The lunar module had two stages. The lower one, the descent stage, was an eight-sided box about 4.2 m across, with four legs holding it 1.5 m above the ground. Apollo 12’s belonged to Intrepid, the second crewed vehicle to land on the Moon, carrying Pete Conrad and Alan Bean.

    The landing

    A deep-throttling engine, up to about 45,000 newtons, sat on a swiveling mount so the pilot could steer a gentle touchdown. Apollo 12 also had a special test: land within about 180 m of Surveyor 3, a probe that had arrived two years earlier. They did.

    The walk

    Conrad and Bean set up the ALSEP, walked to Surveyor 3 and brought home about 10 kg of its parts, plus 34 kg of Moon rock and soil. The parts showed the landing exhaust had thrown dust fast enough to sandblast the old probe.

    The launch

    The descent stage carried the supplies, then served as the launch pad. After 31 h 31 min on the Moon, the ascent stage lifted off on 20 November and the descent stage stayed behind.

    Still there

    It sits at about 3.01° S, 23.42° W in the Ocean of Storms, beside Surveyor Crater. All six crewed descent stages remain near the lunar equator, and LRO photographed them in 2009 and 2011. With Artemis aiming to land astronauts as early as 2028, Apollo 12’s lessons about dust matter again.

      Sojourner: the rover that was supposed to last a week

      The bet

      After Viking, Mars exploration paused for nearly 15 years. Mars Pathfinder was a test: could a new parachute-and-airbag landing deliver a lander and a free-roaming rover? Sojourner rode folded inside it. NASA named it after Sojourner Truth, an abolitionist and women’s rights champion, chosen from a student essay by 12-year-old Valerie Ambroise.

      The landing

      On 4 July 1997, Pathfinder bounced to a stop on Ares Vallis, an ancient flood plain picked for its mix of rocks left by a giant flood. On sol 2 the rover rolled down the ramp. It was the first wheeled vehicle operated on another planet.

      The machine

      About 65 cm long and 10.5 kg, with six wheels on a rocker-bogie suspension that rolled over obstacles about a wheel tall. A 0.22 m² solar panel gave about 16 watts, backed by nine non-rechargeable batteries. It crept at about 1 cm per second and talked only to the lander, which relayed to Earth.

      What it found

      Its X-ray spectrometer read the elements in rocks and soil at 16 spots, and they resembled volcanic rocks on Earth. With the lander, it found rounded pebbles and cobbles that suggest running water long ago, so Mars was once warmer and wetter. Controllers nicknamed rocks after cartoon characters: Barnacle Bill, Yogi, Scooby Doo.

      The silence

      Sojourner never strayed more than about 12 m from the lander. On 27 September 1997, sol 83, the last data arrived, when the lander’s power system failed and cut the rover off from Earth. Teams tried for five months to restore contact and closed the mission on 10 March 1998.

      Still there

      Sojourner is parked beside the lander, renamed the Carl Sagan Memorial Station. Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter photographed the lander in 2006. Its twin, Marie Curie, is at the Smithsonian. Its airbags and suspension were scaled up for Spirit and Opportunity, and it started the line that led to Curiosity and Perseverance.
